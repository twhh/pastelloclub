The moment a baby arrives, the account suggestions begin. Grandparents mention savings bonds. An app ad mentions custodial accounts. Your bank emails about "future planning." But for most families there are really only two accounts competing for the same spare $25 a month: the brand-new federal one, and the one your state has been nudging you toward for years. (New to 529s entirely? [We wrote a plain-English guide](/posts/529-plan-for-new-parents/).)

So let's actually compare them. The Trump Account and the 529 are not rivals as much as they're different tools - but your budget doesn't care about that distinction, so this note will make the call anyway.

The two-minute version, if you're feeding a baby with one hand: claim the Trump Account no matter what, because it comes with $1,000 of federal money and [claiming it takes about twenty minutes](/posts/trump-account-claim-guide/). Then, if education savings is your goal, the 529 usually earns your first recurring dollars - especially if your state gives a tax deduction for contributing. Fund both if you can. The free money first, always.

## The Account That Didn't Exist Last Year

Quick recap: Trump Accounts are starter savings accounts created by the July 2025 tax law. Kids born between 2025 and 2028 get a one-time $1,000 federal deposit once a parent claims it. Families can add up to $5,000 a year of their own money, which grows invested.

The defining feature is the lock. Nothing comes out before the year your child turns 18 - not for a roof repair, not for a market crash, not for tuition in a pinch. At 18 the account becomes your kid's, structured like a retirement account. It is, by design, money that survives everyone's worst impulses, including a teenager's.

If the point of your savings is "my child's life-start, long-term," this is that account.

## The Account Your State Wants You to Use

The 529 has been around since the nineties, and it exists for one job: education. Money goes in after tax, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free for qualified education costs - college tuition, room and board, books, and up to $10,000 a year for K-12 tuition. More than thirty states add a sweetener: a state income tax deduction or credit for contributions.

California, where we are, is not one of them - we checked, hoping otherwise. Our ScholarShare account did open with a small welcome bonus the plan dropped in for free, which was a nice hello, but the ongoing state tax break just isn't there for Californians. (Sacramento keeps proposing one; as of this writing it hasn't landed.)

It's more flexible than its reputation. The beneficiary can change - saved for kid one who got a scholarship? Redirect to kid two, or even yourself. And since 2024, leftover 529 money can roll into the child's Roth IRA - up to $35,000 over time, once the account has been open fifteen years - which quietly turns an education account into a retirement head start.

The trade-off: pull money out for non-education reasons and the earnings get taxed plus a 10% penalty. It's flexible, not free-form.

## The Table That Settles Most of It

| Question | Trump Account | 529 |
|---|---|---|
| What it's for | The kid's adulthood - a long-term head start | Education, kindergarten through college |
| The free money | $1,000 federal seed (2025-2028 births) | None, but many states give a tax deduction |
| Who controls it | The kid, at 18 | You, indefinitely |
| Early access | Locked until the year they turn 18 | Yes, but earnings face taxes plus a 10% penalty |
| If it's never needed | Keeps compounding toward retirement | Roth rollover (up to $35k), or change beneficiaries |

## The Question Nobody Mentions: Control

Here's the difference that doesn't show up in any comparison chart but matters most to some parents: at 18, the Trump Account hands the keys to your child. The 529 never does - you stay the account owner and can redirect the money until you're gone.

Whether the handoff is a feature or a bug depends on the kid and on you. Some parents love it: the account becomes a real lesson in money, and the lock prevents a graduation-week splurge. Others want veto power for another decade or two. Neither instinct is wrong - but walk in knowing which account takes which side.

## The Flexibility Gap

Life will not follow your savings plan. When it doesn't, the 529 has doors: swap beneficiaries, pay for K-12, roll up to $35,000 into a Roth, or take the penalty when life truly demands it. The Trump Account has a wall: the money waits, and in rare tragic cases (like the death of a child) there are provisions, but there is no tuition escape hatch.

Again - the wall is the product. The entire bet is that money nobody can touch for eighteen years becomes money that changes a life. Just don't put your emergency fund in it, because it isn't one.

## The Order We're Doing It

For our family, the sequence came out like this:

1. **Claim the Trump Account for every kid who qualifies.** Free $1,000 outranks every strategy ever devised. Twenty minutes, no contribution required.
2. **Education dollars go to the 529 first.** If your state gives a deduction, this ordering is close to automatic. Ours doesn't - California offers no deduction for ScholarShare - so for us it's the flexibility that tips it: the 529 fits the way life actually goes with kids. If your state also skips the deduction, this is still the order we'd use, just with less margin.
3. **Small monthly dollars into the Trump Account when there's room.** Not every month. It's the kid's "start," not our savings plan. ([Run your numbers in the calculator](/tools/trump-account-calculator/) before deciding on an amount.)

Your order may differ - a family certain about private school leans 529 even harder; a family with no education worries may skip it entirely. The point of writing the order down is that the debate ends and the transfers start.

Because the best account for the first $25 is the one that actually receives it. Claim the free thousand this week. Pick the recurring account this month. Then stop researching and let the boring part - time - do its job.
