A quiet strategy has been making the rounds with parents of teenagers: open a Trump Account for a 17-year-old, drop in $5,000 once, and at 18 convert the whole thing to a Roth IRA. The kid ends up with five figures of tax-free retirement money before their first real job. Who wouldn't want that?

The move is real. The viral version of it skips one paragraph of tax code, and that paragraph is the whole ballgame. Here's both parts.

## Why the Move Works at All

Two features make it possible. First, older kids qualify: any child under 18 can have a Trump Account, even without the $1,000 federal seed (that's reserved for [2025-2028 births](/posts/trump-account-claim-guide/)). Contributions are capped at $5,000 a year - so a parent of a 17-year-old gets exactly one shot at the max before the window closes.

Second, at 18 the account doesn't just sit there. It automatically becomes a Traditional IRA in your child's name, and they can elect to convert it to a Roth instead ([the full mechanics](/posts/trump-account-what-happens-at-18/)). A Roth conversion means paying income tax now, at your kid's presumably tiny rate, in exchange for never paying tax again. Done right, that's the best trade in personal finance.

## The Catch: Your 18-Year-Old Is Probably Still Your Dependent

This is the paragraph the viral posts skip.

A Roth conversion counts as taxable income, and everyone quotes the same attractive fact: the standard deduction is around $15,000, so a $5,000 conversion should be tax-free. That number is real - for a single adult who isn't anyone's dependent.

Your 18-year-old, living at home, claimed on your tax return? Different rules:

- A dependent's standard deduction is roughly **$1,500** (the greater of a small floor or their earned income plus a few hundred dollars - 2025 numbers, indexed yearly).
- Investment-type income above roughly **$2,600 a year** gets hit with the **kiddie tax**: taxed at *your* marginal rate, not the kid's 10% bracket.
- The kiddie tax still applies at 18 and beyond as long as their earned income stays under half their support - which, for a college freshman, it does.

Run the popular version of the move - convert $5,000 plus growth in one year, no other income - and the first ~$1,500 is sheltered, the next ~$1,100 is taxed gently in the child's bracket, and everything above ~$2,600 is taxed at your rate. If you're in a high bracket, you just donated a surprising chunk of the "free" conversion back to the IRS.

## Three Ways to Do the Move Right

**1. Spread the conversion across years.** Convert $2,500 or so per year over two or three years, staying under the kiddie-tax threshold each time. Slower, but each slice lands tax-free or near it.

**2. Wait until the kid is off your return.** The year they're no longer your dependent - graduated, self-supporting - they get the full standard deduction, and a conversion up to it owes roughly zero. For many families this beats rushing it at 18. (The five-year rule on converted Roth money matters for early withdrawals, not for the tax due, so waiting rarely hurts.)

**3. Pair the conversion with earned income.** The summer your kid has a real paycheck, their standard deduction and their kiddie-tax exposure both improve. A working 19-year-old converting a modest amount can thread the needle cleanly.

The right pick depends on your bracket, their income, and their path - which is a spreadsheet conversation, not a TikTok one.

## Is the Move Worth It Without the Optimization?

Honestly, usually yes. Even a sloppily-timed conversion leaves a teenager with a Roth IRA that compounds tax-free for fifty years. The difference between the viral version and the careful version is a few hundred dollars of tax now - meaningful, not fatal.
