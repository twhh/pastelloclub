## The Short Version

The [Tripp Trapp](https://amzn.to/3TCDAd6) is the high chair that adjusts instead of getting outgrown: the seat and footrest move as the kid grows, and the chair holds up to 200 pounds. We bought the bundle for our first, used it from the newborn days through four-year-old dinners, and liked it enough to buy a second chair for the infant. It's still furniture we're happy to have in the dining room - which no other baby gear can say.

## Why We Brought It Home

When we were registering, the Tripp Trapp was the only chair on the market that genuinely ran newborn through childhood - most high chairs start at six months and retire by three years, becoming garage donations. This one starts with a newborn attachment that cradles a baby at table height, then converts to a toddler seat, then just... stays. The rated capacity is 200 pounds. Our four-year-old sits in hers for every dinner.

Two things sealed it beyond the lifecycle math: it matched our dining room furniture (this is a real wooden chair, not a plastic event), and it looked like something built to survive. Four years in, that's held up - we adjust the height every so often, and the chair does the rest.

The second kid got [just the chair](https://link.amazon/B03gUd5qI) - the attachments from the first chair transfer, so the newborn set we loved the first time is now on chair number two. One bundle, two kids.

## The Everyday Feel

The newborn attachment is the sleeper feature. A newborn at the dinner table sounds like a gimmick; in practice it means the baby naps through dinner at eye level with the family instead of in a swing in another room. That attachment worked great for us both times.

After the newborn stage, the chair becomes the kid's spot. Our four-year-old's seat and footrest have climbed three settings in four years - each adjustment takes an Allen key and a few minutes, and between adjustments you forget the chair is "baby gear" at all. It's just her chair, next to ours.

## A Few Quiet Details

- **The adjustability is the product.** Seat and footrest move independently, so the kid always has feet supported - which is the ergonomics pitch of the whole design.
- **It looks like furniture.** Ours sits at the dining table full-time and reads as a wood chair, not equipment. Guests routinely don't clock it as a high chair until a kid is in it.
- **The storage accessory** ([hooks onto the chair's back rail](https://link.amazon/B0i7fovuz)) keeps bibs and wipes within reach.
- **The [baby set](https://link.amazon/B0hERNpWS)** bridges newborn attachment to big-kid seat - the rail-and-harness stage that keeps a wiggly one-year-old contained.

## The Low Lights

- **Height adjustments are a hassle.** Loosening, repositioning, re-tightening slats takes real minutes and a key. It's rare enough not to matter - you'll adjust a handful of times per childhood - but it's nobody's favorite chore.
- **Mild paint chipping after a couple of years**, mostly around the edges where little hands and little feet grip. Cosmetic, but visible if you're picky.
- **The tray attachment's clip was too tight - and it snapped.** Ours broke during the baby-set era; the chair itself was unaffected. We've used it chair-at-the-table ever since, so the loss was smaller than it sounds, but a snapped part is a snapped part.

## Is It Worth the Space?

We own two, which is the strongest review we can give. The first chair has run newborn-to-four and shows only chips; the second is starting the same journey with hand-me-down attachments. Price per year of use, the math beats any plastic high chair we considered - those get 18 months before they're done.

If you're registering: the bundle (chair + newborn set) is the move for a first kid. Second kid, chair only. And if your dining room has real furniture in it, this is the one piece of baby gear that belongs in the room.
