When you leave a salaried job to work for yourself, the salary is the part everyone talks about. The part that actually keeps you up at 2 a.m. is the benefits packet - the one that disappears the day you do.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: you don't have to rebuild the whole packet. You have three doors. You can **build** benefits yourself, piecemeal, as the self-employed person. You can **buy** them retail on the marketplace. Or you can **borrow** them through your spouse's employer, the same way families have done it for decades - just in reverse, with the self-employed parent riding on the W-2 side of the house.

We went through all three doors five years ago, when I left a product job to start a consulting LLC and our first kid was on the way. Here's the actual math, in the order we ran it.

## The Math That Decided It

Before I went solo, my wife and I were both on my company's plan - hers was the worse of the two. When I left, we switched to hers, warts and all.

The numbers made the decision for us. Adding the family to her employer plan runs about **$200 per person** for health, dental, and vision bundled. Pricing the same coverage on Covered California and the federal marketplace came back at **easily over $500 per person**.

Run that out for a family of four and you're choosing between roughly $800 a month and $2,000+ a month - about a **$14,000-a-year gap** - for insurance that, at the end of the day, sees the same doctors and fills the same prescriptions. Her plan was objectively worse than the corporate plan I'd given up. It still won by a mile. A worse plan that costs $14,000 less per year isn't a worse plan.

## What I Checked Before Deciding

I didn't take the spouse plan on faith. Before committing, I priced three routes:

- **Covered California and the marketplace.** Best case for full family coverage: $500+ per person in premiums, before deductibles. In a swing-income year, "maybe $24,000 in premiums" is a number you feel in your chest.
- **Group insurance for solo business owners.** This is the option almost nobody knows exists - group plans that cover single-person companies. The catch, when I checked: you generally need operating history to qualify. I was brand new, with an LLC younger than my daughter's due date. No.
- **The new-wave solo plans.** Companies building health coverage specifically for self-employed people. Five years ago most of them barely existed; the ones that did weren't ready for a family of four.

That third door is worth revisiting, and I am - more on that below.

## What I Still Build Myself

The spouse's plan covers health, dental, and vision. The rest of the benefits packet doesn't come with it, so I build those pieces directly:

**Term life insurance.** A 30-year term policy through Protective Life at **$57 a month**. I shopped it against half a dozen carriers, and it beat the group rate I'd had through my old employer - group policies are priced for the average employee, and you are not the average employee. My rate came in well under typical for my coverage because I bought young and healthy, back when I was running half marathons and Spartan races instead of sprinting after a toddler. If you're reading this between training blocks: the price of waiting is real. Buy the policy while your resting heart rate is doing the negotiating for you.

**Retirement, with matching, from myself.** An LLC taxed as an S corporation with a Solo 401(k), where the employer side makes contributions too - I match myself. Two hats, one transfer. The mechanics are in [the money checklist we ran for our second baby](/posts/self-employed-parent-money-checklist/).

**The back office.** Quarterly taxes, payroll, bookkeeping - the unglamorous benefits-adjacent plumbing - run through [Collective](https://share.collective.com/simplybootstrapped). That's a referral link, and also just the service that's handled it for five years.

## The Benefit That Quietly Matters Most

Here's the one that gets skipped in every "self-employed benefits" guide: the **dependent care FSA**.

A self-employed person cannot open one. There's no solo version - it only exists through a W-2 employer's payroll. My wife's employer offers one, so we max it out, and it's the single most reliable tax break in our stack: contributions come out pre-tax and pay for childcare directly. The federal cap is $5,000 a year per household, which sounds meaningful until you see our daycare number.

So here's the receipt: **$3,200 a month** for infant care in the Bay Area. That's the good price - friends were paying $4,000 for comparable care. The cheaper centers had waitlists over a year long, and five years later we're still on one of them, somewhere in the queue, presumably behind families who signed up before their child was a twinkle.

Against $38,000-a-year childcare, a $5,000 pre-tax account doesn't solve anything. It just quietly claws back a slice of it - every year, automatically, through a benefit that only exists because one adult in the house has a regular employer. If that adult is your spouse, the dependent care FSA belongs on the "reasons the spouse plan is load-bearing" list.

## The HSA We Gave Up

I used to have an HSA and loved it - the triple tax advantage is real, and for a healthy single person it's the best account in the tax code.

The catch with the spouse plan: the family enrolls together, and everyone ends up on the same plan. The HSA-eligible option meant high deductibles for two kids, including a newborn whose pediatrician we'd be seeing constantly. We picked the boring, predictable PPO instead and gave up the HSA.

On paper, that's leaving tax-advantaged money on the table. With an infant, "predictable" was worth more than "optimal." If your kids are older and your family rarely sees a doctor, run the high-deductible math yourself - the HSA might be the better trade for you. Ours was a judgment call, and I'd make it again.

## If You're Planning the Leap

Our transition had no horror story - no gap in coverage, no COBRA cliff, no missed month. That's not luck. It's the one part of the plan I'd insist on for anyone:

Open enrollment happens once a year. If you're going solo, find out when your spouse's open enrollment is, and time the leap so you land inside it. Leaving a job mid-year with no spouse plan and no open-enrollment window means bridging with COBRA (expensive) or marketplace coverage (more expensive), and the people who eat that cost are usually the ones who quit on a Tuesday.

Plan the resignation letter around the insurance calendar. It's the least romantic sentence in this post and possibly the most useful.

## The Order We'd Do It In

For a self-employed parent starting this decision today:

1. **Price the spouse's employer plan first.** In our math it won by thousands a year, even being the "worse" plan. It usually does.
2. **Run the marketplace quotes anyway.** You need the real comparison number, and subsidies change the math at lower incomes.
3. **Buy term life independently, while young and healthy.** Not through any employer - employer group rates are for the average employee, and you're shopping for yourself.
4. **Max the dependent care FSA if anyone in the household has W-2 employment.** It's the benefit that only exists on the other side of the fence.
5. **Set up the Solo 401(k) and automate it.** You're the employer now; be a generous one.
6. **Re-check the group and solo options every year.** The market for self-employed coverage is getting better - companies like Solo Health didn't exist when I checked five years ago. Now that the LLC has grown, I'm pricing coverage through the business for next year. The spouse plan is winning so far, but the gap is closing, and for the first time it's an actual decision.

The benefits packet you lose by going solo is real, and so is the version you can rebuild around it. Ours is mostly borrowed, partly built, and better than the one I left - because this time, every piece of it was chosen on purpose.
