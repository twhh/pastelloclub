The first tax return I filed as a consultant had a line in it that changed the whole plan. I'd left a salaried job, income was swinging, and the numbers said the business might not beat the paycheck I'd given up. Then the software applied the qualified business income deduction, and the bill dropped by an amount I had not budgeted for. That deduction is a real reason consulting stuck.

This post is the explainer I wish I'd had that first year: what QBI is, the income trap nobody warned me about, and how it interacts with the S-corp salary, the Solo 401(k) match, and the home office - because they all pull on the same dollars.

## What QBI Actually Is

The qualified business income deduction - Section 199A, if you want to sound impressive at parties - lets most self-employed people deduct **20% of their business profit** before arriving at their final taxable income. Sole proprietors, LLCs, partnerships, S corps: the profit passes through to your personal return either way, and QBI shaves a fifth of it off before the tax math finishes.

Two things it is not. It's a deduction, not a credit - it shrinks the income you're taxed on, not the tax bill itself. And it doesn't apply to everything: W-2 wages, capital gains, and interest income don't count. Business profit does.

Run a concrete number: **$100,000 of consulting profit, and QBI knocks $20,000 off taxable income** before the standard deduction even shows up. At a 24% federal bracket that's roughly $4,800 back - real money in a year when you're deciding whether the solo thing works.

Two updates from the 2025 tax law worth knowing: the deduction is now permanent (it had been scheduled to sunset), and there's a small minimum deduction for very low-profit years, so a lean year still gets something.

## The Catch That Surprised Me

Several years in, I learned the hard way that not all business income stays qualified.

Consulting - like law, medicine, financial advising, and most work where the product is you - counts as a **specified service trade or business** under the rules. SSTBs get QBI in full below an income threshold. Above it, the deduction phases out over a range and then disappears completely.

For 2026, the phase-out starts at **$394,600 of taxable income** married filing jointly (fully gone about $150,000 later), or **$197,300** single (fully gone about $79,000 later). A swing-income consultant can cross it in a good year without feeling richer - the big year shows up, and the QBI line on the return simply isn't there anymore.

Nobody told me that in year one. The deduction that made the business viable at six figures quietly stops applying in the years the business does best, unless you're above the line for other reasons (like large retirement contributions pulling taxable income back down - one more reason to max that Solo 401(k), covered below).

## The S-corp Wrinkle

If your LLC is taxed as an S corp - the setup [our benefits post](/posts/self-employed-benefits-decision/) and [our Solo 401(k) comparison](/posts/solo-401k-vs-sep-ira/) assume - QBI gets more interesting, because an S corp pays you two ways and they don't treat QBI the same:

- **Salary (W-2 wages):** not qualified business income. Every dollar of salary reduces your QBI dollar for dollar. It does carry payroll taxes, and it's the base for the **employer-side 401(k) contribution** - the "I match myself" move from the Solo 401(k) post.
- **Distributions:** qualified business income. No payroll tax, and they keep your QBI deduction intact.

So the salary decision is a three-way balance: pay yourself too little and the IRS can recharacterize distributions as wages (reasonable compensation is a requirement, not a suggestion); pay yourself too much and you burn payroll tax and QBI on dollars that didn't need either. Meanwhile the salary is exactly what powers the 25% employer 401(k) match - [the calculator shows that trade at your income](/tools/solo-401k-calculator/).

What each dollar does, side by side:

| | Payroll tax | Counts for QBI | Powers the 401(k) match |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salary dollar | Yes | No | Yes - it's the base |
| Distribution dollar | No | Yes | No |
| Home-office reimbursement | No | N/A (tax-free either way) | No |

## How I Set the Salary

I skipped the folk formulas ("40% of profit," "whatever feels defensible") and used **market compensation data for my actual role** - what employers pay for the work I do, at my level, in my market. The salary is the job; the distributions are the business's profit on top of it. That framing passes the reasonableness test cleanly and gives me a number I can point at if anyone ever asks.

One habit worth stealing: revisit the salary when the business changes, not on a calendar. Income that swings means the right salary two years ago can be indefensibly low today.

## The Short Version

1. **QBI is 20% of business profit off your taxable income** - permanent as of the 2025 law, and the reason year one of consulting penciled out.
2. **Service businesses lose it above an income line** ($394,600 MFJ in 2026, fully gone ~$150k later). Know where your good years land.
3. **Salary reduces QBI but powers the 401(k) match; distributions keep QBI.** Set the salary with market comp data for the role, and revisit it when the business changes.
4. **Automate the rest** - payroll, quarterlies, and the home-office reimbursement are plumbing, not a hobby.

The year-one version of me needed exactly one sentence: your best tax moves as a solo consultant are the ones you set up in advance, and QBI is the one that starts working before you've optimized anything.
