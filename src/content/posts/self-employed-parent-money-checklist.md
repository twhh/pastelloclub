When our friends have babies at salaried jobs, a packet shows up. Someone from HR schedules a call. Benefits get adjusted, leave gets counted, a 401(k) keeps humming along without anyone touching it. It's not magic, but it's a system.

When you run the business, you are the system. There is no packet. There's a newborn, a laptop you're afraid to open, and the quiet realization that nobody is going to handle any of this for you.

Two kids into running a household and a small business, we've settled on a checklist. It's not clever - most of money never is - but it's the six things that actually matter when a baby and a business arrive at the same time.

## Count Your Runway Before Anything Else

Self-employed income doesn't dip, it swings. And a baby turns a normal swing month into a scary one - daycare hits the same month a client pays late, and your health insurance premium (which you're paying entirely yourself) doesn't care about either.

So the first number to know isn't your income. It's your runway: how many months of must-spend money - housing, food, insurance, the business's bare bones - you have in cash. We aim for six months. Some seasons we've hit three. The point isn't the exact number; it's that you've counted, because counted fear is manageable and uncounted fear just hums in the background while you're trying to enjoy a newborn.

## File for the Credits That Feel Like a Maze

The tax code has kids built into it, but the benefits don't file themselves. The big ones to know by name:

- **The child tax credit** - the headline credit most parents know, worth up to $2,000 per kid depending on income.
- **The child and dependent care credit** - daycare, babysitting while you work, even a nanny's share can count. If you're paying for care so you can run the business, this credit is partly for exactly that.

The self-employed wrinkles are real (some credits, and the retirement side of the code, have special versions for people who are their own employer), and this is the one section of the checklist where I'll happily say: pay a tax professional. Ours finds more in an hour than she costs in a year, mostly because she knows which boxes a generalist misses.

## Claim the Free $1,000

New since last year: babies born 2025 through 2028 get a one-time $1,000 federal deposit in a new Trump Account - but only if a parent [claims it](/posts/trump-account-claim-guide/). It takes about twenty minutes at the government's portal, it doesn't require you to contribute anything of your own, and there is no version of a tight budget where free money doesn't fit.

If you're also weighing education savings, [we compared the Trump Account against the 529 here](/posts/trump-account-vs-529/) - short version: claim the free thousand this week, decide the rest later.

## Keep Retirement on Autopilot

Nobody matches your contributions when you're the employer. There's no payroll deduction quietly doing the work. What self-employed families get instead is a genuinely good menu - Solo 401(k)s, SEP plans - and total freedom to ignore it, which is exactly what happens when a baby arrives.

Our own setup, for the curious: an LLC in California taxed as an S corporation, with a Solo 401(k). The best quirk of the arrangement is that the S corp side makes employer contributions into my Solo 401(k) - I match myself. Two hats, one transfer. (The admin for all of it - S corp filing, bookkeeping, payroll, quarterly taxes - runs through [Collective](https://share.collective.com/simplybootstrapped); that's a referral link, and also just the service I've used for five years.)

So: automate something small. Even $50 a month, transferred on the same day, on schedule, before you can see it. The lean months will attack your retirement savings before they attack anything else - that's just how it goes - and the only defense is a transfer that happens without a decision being made.

## Insure the Paycheck You Are

If you work for yourself, your family's financial plan has a single point of failure, and it's you. Two policies cover it:

- **Term life insurance** - replaces your income for your family if the worst happens. It's cheapest while you're young and healthy, which is exactly when it feels most unnecessary.
- **Disability insurance** - replaces your income if you can't work, which is far more likely than the worst, and hits self-employed families hardest because there's no employer plan underneath you.

We got quotes during the first pregnancy and put off signing until the second was on the way - classic. Do as we finally did, not as we did. Twenty minutes of quotes, mostly done from the nursery floor.

## One Bucket, One Day a Month

Everything kid-related - the 529, the Trump Account contributions, gifts from grandparents - flows through one labeled account, and the transfers happen on the first of the month like a bill. It's not optimization. It's not even particularly smart. It's a habit that survives exhaustion, which is the only kind of habit that survives having both a business and a baby.

Once a month, on the same day: move the money, glance at the runway number, close the laptop. The whole ritual takes ten minutes.

## The Whole Point

The checklist's real job isn't to make you richer. It's to make money boring - settled enough that you can go back to the two things that actually need you: the baby, and the business, and okay, the dog too.

Count the runway. Claim the credits. Take the free thousand. Automate the future. Insure yourself. Then get back to the good part.
